## Claim

Taylorism organizes work by breaking it into small-granularity tasks, each
performed by a highly specialized worker — and it genuinely worked, credited
here as a real driver of the industrial revolution. It worked because early
manufacturing assembly required few steps, each simple enough that a worker
needed no context about what came before: handovers between specialized
workers were cheap. Applying that same fine-grained decomposition to modern,
information-heavy organizations fails, because modern process steps are
interrelated and each one needs context on the *whole case*, not just the
task in front of the worker. Every handover now forces the receiving worker
to acquire that context from scratch, and this handover cost — not task
difficulty — becomes the dominant inefficiency. The resolution process
orientation offers: combine small units of work back into larger-granularity
units, run by **knowledge workers** — workers with broad skills and a broad
understanding of the work's ultimate goal, not narrow specialists — which
reduces the number of handovers a case has to survive.

## Why it matters to a viewer

This is the section's most self-contained argument: a clear villain (naive
fine-grained decomposition), a clear mechanism (handover cost, paid every
time context has to be reacquired), and a clear resolution (coarser work
units plus knowledge workers). It also rewards a viewer who's already seen
Chapter 1's knowledge-worker material — the same figure reappears here, now
with the specific failure mode that makes broad skills necessary rather than
just nice to have. Worth a one-line callback to that earlier material, but
the callback isn't the point; the point is the mechanism.
