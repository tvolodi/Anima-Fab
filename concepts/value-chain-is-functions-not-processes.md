## Claim

A **value chain** (Porter) is one enterprise's internal structure, represented
as a set of coarse-grained business functions that can be broken down into
finer-grained ones — functional decomposition. A **value system** is the
larger ecology: the related value chains of multiple cooperating enterprises,
each pursuing its own business goals while depending on the others. Within a
single value chain (Fig 2.11), functions split into **primary functions** —
Inbound Logistics, Operations, Outbound Logistics, Marketing and Sales,
Services — which contribute directly to competitive advantage, and **support
functions** — Firm Infrastructure, Human Resource Management, Technology
Management, Procurement — which create the environment that lets the primary
functions run efficiently. **Margin** is what's left over: the difference
between resources invested and revenue generated, and every function, primary
or support, has to contribute to it. But Weske names an explicit gap in
Porter's model (¶75-76): it decomposes a company's work into functions very
well, but it does not identify the role of *processes* — even though
processes fit naturally into the value-chain approach. Supplying that missing
layer, relating the business functions a value chain identifies to the
business processes that actually realize them, is the job the rest of the
chapter takes on.

## Why it matters to a viewer

Without this beat, "value chain" reads as a static org-chart diagram — boxes
with labels. Framing it as functions-without-processes turns it into a setup
with a payoff still to come: the viewer is told, explicitly, that something
is missing from the picture they're looking at, and that the next section
exists to fill exactly that hole. It also gives a clean two-axis way to sort
any business activity a viewer might name: is it primary or support, and does
it move the needle on Margin. (Fig 2.9's value-system diagram is a loose
communicative convention, not a formal ordering of activities — worth one
cautionary line if the diagram is shown, not a claim of its own.)
